Many transition efforts fail between vision and implementation. The coalition forms, the pilot launches, the funding arrives, and then the actual coordination work becomes brittle: meetings, spreadsheets, ad hoc authority, platform workarounds, and reports that flatten the living system they are meant to serve.

Neighbourhoods is a response to that missing middle. It gives communities a way to define the forms through which they coordinate, adapt, remember, and interoperate.

## Structure without standardisation

Communities need structure. They also need local agency. Centralised systems produce coherence by standardising practice; fully local systems preserve autonomy but struggle to interoperate. Grammars create a third possibility: shared patterns that can be reused, forked, and adapted without making all communities the same.

A civic coalition, soil network, care economy, cooperative federation, or research commons can each define the valid moves of its work and still remain legible to others.

## AI with boundaries

AI can generate interfaces, summaries, plans, and agents. It cannot be the source of truth for a social system. Neighbourhoods places AI above the grammar/runtime layer: AI may help a community express itself, but the grammar determines what is valid.

That distinction matters for systems change. It allows generative tools to support living systems without quietly replacing their governance.

## Funding the pattern, not only the project

Systems funders often support pilots that each rebuild the same operational layer. Neighbourhoods makes coordination patterns fundable. A grammar for mutual aid, participatory budgeting, regenerative land stewardship, cooperative governance, or community finance can be improved once and reused many times.

The leverage is not only in one deployment. It is in the pattern that becomes shared infrastructure.
